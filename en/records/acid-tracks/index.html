Why this record

It shows, in its purest form, how an instrument designed for one job becomes something else entirely in the hands of young DJs.

How it was made

Twelve minutes of TB-303 whose cutoff, resonance and envelope knobs are twisted non-stop over a bone-dry 707 and 727. The melody barely matters: everything happens in the mutating timbre, a bassline that "talks", squeals and chokes.

Origins and production approach

DJ Pierre, Earl "Spanky" Smith Jr. and Herb J tinkered with a TB-303 bought for around $40 without knowing how to use it. The accident became the method: Spanky programmed, Pierre tweaked the settings until that sound appeared. They recorded it at around 128 BPM; Marshall Jefferson told them to slow it down, and the track settled at 120.

Club sociology

Ron Hardy played the tape several times in one night at the Music Box. The crowd called it "Ron Hardy's Acid Track"; the group kept the nickname, dropped the DJ's name, and the record came out on Trax in 1987 as "Acid Tracks".

Ballroom & queer scenes

Acid was born in the Black and gay space of the Music Box. Once it crossed the Atlantic, it was re-appropriated by Britain's white rave youth, and the smiley partly erased its origins: a textbook case of cultural uprooting.
