Why this record

The bridge between house and techno: orchestral emotion carried by the machine.

How it was made

Sampled strings and piano (the piano comes from an improvisation by Michael James) cut into syncopated riffs over a relentless 909. The absence of a conventional bassline creates constant tension, like an orchestra in free fall.

Roland TR-909 Rhythm ComposerEnsoniq Mirage (sampler)

Origins and production approach

Derrick May, one of the "Belleville Three" with Juan Atkins and Kevin Saunderson, came from a Black suburb of Detroit. He saw the machine as a way out of the post-industrial city and a means of projecting himself into the future.

Club sociology

Detroit collapsed as the car industry left. The Music Institute (1988–1989) and parties thrown by Black middle-class high-schoolers formed the scene. In Europe, the record became a rave anthem.

Ballroom & queer scenes

The techno narrative has often erased its queer debts, especially to Ken Collier, the Black gay Detroit DJ whose sets schooled a whole generation. Detroit's "futurity" also rests on that memory.
